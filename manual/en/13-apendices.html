A.6 Files the app produces

In the .xlsx workbooks, each table takes its own sheet; sheet names follow the language of the app, and some column headers use fixed symbols or abbreviations (sigma2A, SE, GEBV). InIntroduction to BreedingPro

✓Decision rule · which block to useSection I.8
If the table has the female and male of a group of parents crossed among themselves…then Griffing (4), Hayman–Jinks (5) and parameters (8).
If males and females are different groups (NC, L × T) or the diallel is partial…then mating designs (6) and parameters (8).
If there is a generation column (P₁, P₂, F₁, F₂…)…then generations (7).
If they are genotypes in several locations or years…then parameters (8), stability (9) and, with a pedigree, BLUP (10).
If you have a pedigree or markers…then BLUP (10) or genomics (11).

Block 3 makes this choice for you when it recognizes the design, and states it on the 3 · Recognized design card.

✓Reading rule · a P valueSection I.9
If P < 0.001 (***)…then very strong evidence.
If 0.001 ≤ P < 0.01 (**)…then strong evidence.
If 0.01 ≤ P < 0.05 (*)…then moderate evidence.
If P ≥ 0.05 (ns)…then not significant at 5%.

A P value does not measure the importance of an effect: also look at its size and its standard error.

B1Home, laboratories and theory

✓Decision rule · how the app words the interpretationSection 1.2
If b does not differ from 1 (|t| smaller than the critical value at 5% with p − 2 degrees of freedom)…then the additive–dominance model holds.
If b departs from 1…then there is non-allelic interaction or another broken assumption, and the components are biased.
If (H1/D)½ < 0.25…then gene action is practically additive.
If the intercept of the line is positive and significant…then partial dominance.
If it is negative and significant…then overdominance.
If it does not differ from zero and (H1/D)½ is between 0.8 and 1.2…then complete dominance. Outside that interval, the app warns that the intercept test has little power (Exercise 4).
If r(Yr, Wr + Vr) < −0.3 · > 0.3…then the dominant · recessive alleles increase the trait; between both values the direction is unclear.

The app also names the two parents closest to the origin: they are the ones that carry the most dominant alleles.

B2Planning crosses, trial and power

✓Decision rule · which field design to useSection 2.4
If there are few entries (up to 15 or 20) and the land is uniform over the size of a replicate…then randomized complete blocks.
If there are many entries and a full replicate no longer fits on homogeneous soil…then α-lattice, with blocks of a size close to √v.
If there are many new entries and seed for a single plot of each…then augmented, with at least two checks and 12 or more error degrees of freedom.
If the plots are pots or homogeneous units (greenhouse, laboratory)…then completely randomized.

The app requires at least two replicates, except in the augmented design, and warns when the error is left with fewer than 12 degrees of freedom.

B4Griffing’s diallels

✓Reading rule · which effect is useful for whatSection 4.4
High, significant GCA (ĝi)The parent passes its value on to all its crosses: useful for forming populations and synthetics and for choosing the parents of a program.
High, significant SCA (ŝij)The pair performs better than its GCA effects predict: useful for hybrids of that combination, not for choosing parents.
Maternal effect (mi)Which parent is the female matters: use the parent in the favorable direction (cytoplasm, endosperm, seed size).
Non-maternal reciprocal (nij)The direction matters only in that pair; confirm it before drawing conclusions.

Baker’s ratio sums up the balance: close to 1, GCA predicts the crosses well and additive action dominates (Section 4.5).

B5Hayman–Jinks and Gardner–Eberhart

✓Reading rule · the Wr–Vr graphSection 5.5
Slope that does not depart from 1The additive–dominance model is adequate; if it departs, there is epistasis or genes correlated between parents.
Intercept positive · zero · negativePartial dominance · complete dominance · overdominance.
Distance from the origin of each pointNear: the parent carries mostly dominant alleles; far: recessive ones.
Correlation between the parent’s value and Wr + VrPositive: dominant alleles decrease the trait; negative: they increase it.

B6Mating designs

✓Decision rule · the inbreeding F of the parentsSection 6.2
If the parents are inbred lines (line × tester, partial diallels of lines)…then F = 1.
If they are plants from a population (North Carolina designs, triple test cross)…then F = 0.
If they are lines with few generations of selfing…then F = 0.5 or 0.75, depending on the generation.
If the Block 2 field book declared another value…then the app uses that one: confirm it.

With the same mean squares, going from F = 0 to F = 1 divides σ²A by 2 and σ²D by 4.

✓Reading rule · components and tests of a designSections 6.5 to 6.8
If a component comes out negative…then the true variance is close to zero or replicates are lacking. It is reported as it comes out; with a negative σ²D there is no degree of dominance, H² or Baker’s ratio.
If the two σ²A of a North Carolina II, that of males and that of females, differ greatly…then suspect maternal effects or that males and females do not come from the same population.
If in the triple test cross the contrast L1 + L2 − 2L3 is significant…then there is epistasis, provided L3 really is the F1: check F₁ tester (L₃).
If in line × tester the interaction dominates and σ²A is imprecise…then almost all the variation among crosses is specific; a few purposely chosen testers do not estimate the σ²A of a population.

The test that a component is zero is the F of its source, not its standard error.

B7Generations and heterosis

✓Decision rule · the variance of each meanSection 7.3
If the plants were randomized one by one (or there is one plant per plot)…then the variance among individuals divided by their number, s²/n.
If there are replicated plots of several plants…then the plot error without blocks, which is what automatic does.
If there is a single value per plot…then the residual mean square; there is no D, H, F, E nor effective factors.
If there are plots and among individuals / n is forced…then the standard errors come out too small and any difference looks significant: do not do it.

The blocks do not enter the variance of the means: each generation is in all of them and their effect cancels out in the comparisons.

✓Reading rule · scaling, models and heterosisSections 7.4 to 7.7
If a single scaling test is significant…then the additive–dominance model is not enough; the joint test says which epistasis is needed.
If the scaling tests fail…then before adding epistasis try a transformation of the trait: it may be a scale effect.
If [h] and [l] are significant with the same sign · with opposite signs…then complementary · duplicate epistasis (in the chosen metric).
If D comes out negative…then there are few plants, linkage or epistasis; read Warner’s h² and H² from the F2.
If the question is gene action · whether the F1 is worthwhile…then read heterosis over the parents · standard heterosis against the check.

The difference is what is tested, with its standard error and Satterthwaite degrees of freedom; the percentage only helps to read it.

B8Genetic parameters and selection

✓Reading rule · a heritabilitySections 8.2 to 8.4
If H² on a mean basis < 0.3 · 0.3 to 0.6 · > 0.6…then low · medium · high (the gray band of Figure 8.2).
If Knapp’s interval is wide (few genotypes)…then the class is uncertain: report the interval, not just the value.
If there are several environments…then use the combined H², with σ²GE; that of a single environment mixes the interaction with the genetic part.
If the trial has incomplete blocks or is unbalanced…then Cullis’s H².

With a negative σ²G the heritability is reported as it is, but genetic advance is taken as zero.

✓Decision rule · when to trust path analysisSection 8.6
If the app does not show the table (the matrix is not positive definite or R² > 1)…then use the phenotypic correlations, remove traits with small σ²G or increase the ridge constant.
If the condition number exceeds 100 or there are large VIFs…then the direct effects are unstable even if the matrix is valid.
If a direct effect is small and the total correlation large…then the trait acts through others: its indirect effects tell you so.
✓Decision rule · which selection index to useSections 8.7 and 8.8
If P, G and the economic weights are well estimated…then Smith–Hazel.
If G and P are unreliable…then the base index (b = w).
If a trait must not change…then the restricted index.
If the ratio between gains matters…then the proportional gains index, with θ > 0.
If there are no economic weights…then the desired gains index, or the eigen index (ESIM) for the most heritable combination.
If there is neither P nor G…then the rank-sum of Mulamba and Mock or Elston’s index.

The coefficients are not interpreted one by one: what matters are the expected gains in each trait.

B9Genotype × environment interaction

✓Reading rule · interaction and stabilitySections 9.2 to 9.6
If the environments are a sample of the region · are the only ones of interest…then test the genotypes against the interaction · against the error.
If Bartlett’s test rejects equal errors…then read the two-stage analysis.
If b does not differ from 1 and S²d is not significant…then broad, predictable adaptation; b > 1, it takes advantage of good environments; b < 1, it holds up in poor ones.
If S²d is significant…then the genotype’s response is unpredictable, even if its mean is high.
If you sort by the mean rank of the summary…then use it to order the discussion, not to decide: it adds up statistics that measure different things.

Static stability, in which the genotype yields the same in every environment, is rarely desirable for yield: a genotype that does not change does not take advantage of good environments.

✓Decision rule · AMMI and the GGE biplotSections 9.7 and 9.8
If you must decide how many AMMI axes to retain…then Cornelius’s residual test; Gollob’s is liberal.
If a genotype has a high mean and a low WAAS…then it is desirable: quadrant IV of WAAS against the mean.
If the first two GGE components explain less than 60%…then read the biplot with caution.
If the which-won-where view forms sectors…then they are mega-environments only if the pattern recurs in other years.
If you are looking for a test environment…then one that is long and close to the average-environment axis; a long one with a large angle serves to discard unstable genotypes.

B10Mixed models, BLUP and crossbreeding

✓Decision rule · the mixed modelSections 10.3 and 10.4
If there are hundreds of records · only a few…then estimate the variances by REML · give them as known, from the literature or from a larger population.
If you want to know whether a random effect is needed…then read the likelihood ratio against the ½χ²0 + ½χ²1 mixture; compare only models with the same fixed effects.
If there are many small contemporary groups…then the group as a random effect.
If there are individuals with several records…then add the permanent environment.
If the model exceeds 2,600 unknowns…then trim the pedigree to the ancestors of the individuals with records.
✓Reading rule · breeding values and crossbreedingSections 10.3, 10.5 and 10.6
If the standard error of prediction is as large as the differences…then still choose by breeding value: it already discounts that uncertainty.
If plots are missing, replicates are unequal or the heritability is low…then the trial BLUP beats the mean; with families of few plots, the relationships (K = A) help most.
If you split the additive and non-additive parts with few families…then the split is not reliable: also look at the additive-only model.
If you take the recombination loss from a paper…then use its same convention, /4 or /2.
If a parameter comes out not estimable…then the groups do not separate it: add backcrosses, F2 or three-breed crosses.

B11Genomic selection and hybrid prediction

✓Decision rule · markers and GBLUPSections 11.2 to 11.4
If you have no other criterion for quality control…then the default values: up to 20% missing per marker and per individual, and a MAF of 0.05 or more.
If there are few markers and Block 10 has the pedigree…then blend G with A (Weight of the pedigree).
If an individual hardly resembles its supposed sire genomically…then suspect a pedigree error.
If the genomic heritability comes out lower than that of the trial…then some of the QTL are not in disequilibrium with the panel.
If a marker has a large effect…then it deserves a look, not a conclusion: association is tested with other methods.
✓Reading rule · validation and hybrid predictionSections 11.5 and 11.6
If you want to know how well the model predicts…then read the cross-validation, not the correlation with the individual’s own record, and leave Re-estimate the variances in every training set ticked.
If the slope of the validation is close to 1…then the predictions neither exaggerate nor shrink the differences.
If you compare genomic with phenotypic selection per year…then use the program’s real years per cycle: the ratio depends almost entirely on them.
If T0 drops sharply compared with T2…then the new lines have no close relatives among the evaluated ones.
If two lines are almost identical…then keep one: their crosses will give practically the same.
